Debt Snowball Method Explained with a Real-World Example

Clearpath Team 10 min read

No inheritance. No six-figure income. No side hustle empire. Just a plan, consistency, and eighteen months of showing up every month.

Stories like this one are the most useful thing I can share. Real numbers, real timeline, real mistakes. Read the whole thing before you scroll to the end.

If you are carrying debt right now, I want you to know something. When I started this journey, I was not looking for a perfect financial strategy. I was looking for a way out.

At the time, I had four different debts. None of them seemed catastrophic on their own. But together they felt overwhelming. Every month I made the minimum payments, told myself I would get serious about it later, and then moved on with life.

Eventually, later caught up with me.

One evening, after looking at my bank account and realising how much of my income was disappearing into debt payments, I decided to stop guessing and write everything down.

The reality check

Here is what I was actually dealing with:

DebtBalanceRateMin payment
Store card$70026%$35
Credit card$2,30021%$60
Personal loan$3,00014%$95
Car loan$4,0008%$150

The total came to just over $10,000.

Looking at the numbers in one place was uncomfortable. But it was also strangely relieving. For the first time, I was not avoiding the problem anymore.

After reviewing my spending honestly, I realised I could free up about $260 a month by cutting back and being more intentional. Combined with the minimum payments I was already making, that gave me around $600 a month to put toward debt.

The next step was choosing a strategy.

Why I chose the debt snowball

There are endless debates about the best debt payoff method. Some people prefer the avalanche method because it saves the most money in interest. Others swear by the snowball method because of the early wins.

I chose the snowball.

Not because it was mathematically perfect. Because I knew myself.

I had tried to get out of debt before. Twice. Both times I started strong and eventually lost motivation. This time I needed momentum more than optimisation.

The snowball method works like this. List your debts from smallest balance to largest. Pay the minimum on every one of them. Then throw every spare bit of money at the smallest debt until it is gone. Once it clears, take the whole payment you were making and roll it onto the next one.

My smallest balance was the $700 store card. That became my first target.

The first win changed everything

I threw every extra dollar at the store card.

Around six weeks later, I logged in and saw a zero balance. I actually refreshed the page because I thought something was wrong.

Seven hundred dollars is not life-changing money. But that first victory felt bigger than the amount itself. For years, my debts had only moved in one direction. Seeing one disappear entirely proved that progress was possible.

That small win gave me the confidence to keep going.

"Seven hundred dollars is not life-changing money. But seeing a zero balance for the first time proved that the plan actually worked."

Rolling the snowball forward

Once the store card was gone, the payment I had been making there rolled straight onto the credit card. So instead of $60 a month, the credit card was now receiving much larger payments every month.

What had looked like a balance that would take years started shrinking fast. Month after month, the numbers moved in the right direction.

A few months later, the credit card was gone too.

Two debts down. Two to go. For the first time, I could actually see a finish line.

The hardest part of the journey

The personal loan turned out to be the toughest phase.

Unlike the first two debts, there was no quick win waiting around the corner. There were months where nothing exciting happened. I made the payment, updated my spreadsheet, and waited for next month. Then I repeated the process.

This is the part most debt payoff stories skip over. But it is the part that matters most.

The middle of the journey is where motivation fades. The excitement of starting is gone, and the finish line still feels far away.

The middle is where most people quit. What kept this person going was tracking progress. Even when it did not feel like progress, the numbers showed that it was.

What kept me going was tracking my progress every month. I watched the projected payoff date move closer. Even when I could not feel the momentum, the data showed it was there.

Eventually, the personal loan cleared. Only the car loan remained.

The final stretch

By this point, every payment from the three cleared debts was rolling into the car loan. My snowball had grown significantly.

The $4,000 car loan that had once felt permanent started falling fast. Less than five months later, I made the final payment.

Eighteen months after starting, I was debt free.

What I would do differently

Two things.

First, I would build a small emergency fund before going all-in on debt. During those eighteen months, unexpected expenses showed up more than once. A car repair and a household emergency nearly pushed me back toward using credit. Even a modest cushion would have reduced that stress significantly.

Second, I would have started sooner. The hardest part was not making the payments. It was the years I spent pretending the problem was not as serious as it really was. Avoidance cost me far more than interest ever did.

What actually made the difference

People often assume paying off debt requires extraordinary discipline or a dramatic jump in income.

That was not my experience. Three simple things made all the difference.

Writing down every debt. So I could no longer ignore the total. Seeing it all in one place changed my relationship with the problem.

Getting one small win early. The store card cleared in six weeks. That win proved the system worked. Nothing motivates like proof.

Tracking progress consistently. So I could see momentum even when I could not feel it. The spreadsheet kept me honest and kept me going through the middle.

No secret strategy. No miracle income boost. No overnight transformation. Just a clear plan and enough consistency to follow through.

If you are starting today

If you are looking at your own debts and wondering whether this is actually possible, start exactly where I started.

Write down every debt. The balance. The minimum payment. The rate. Look at the total honestly.

The first step is not paying off the debt. The first step is facing it. Once you do that, you can build a plan. And once you have a plan, progress becomes a lot more predictable than it feels right now.

One payment. One balance. One month at a time.
