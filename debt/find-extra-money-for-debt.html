7 Realistic Ways to Find Extra Money to Pay Off Debt

Oluwaseun Busayo Amusan | Published August 2026 | Updated August 2026 | 9 min read

You do not need a second job or a pay rise. You need to find a little extra from several different places. Here are seven realistic ways to do it, starting with the fastest.

You do not need to find hundreds of dollars. You need to find a little extra from several different places. Here is where to look.

When you are trying to pay off debt, the advice you hear most often is to pay more every month.

Not helpful. You already know that. The question is where that extra money is supposed to come from.

The good news is that you usually do not need to make one dramatic change. You need to find small amounts in a few different places and redirect them consistently. An extra fifty dollars a month does not sound impressive. But applied to a debt balance month after month, it can take months off your payoff timeline and save a meaningful amount in interest.

This article covers seven realistic places to find that extra money, starting with the fastest and least disruptive options.

1. Cancel subscriptions you forgot about

Open your last bank statement or payment history and look at every recurring charge.

Not the obvious ones. The small, automatic ones. A streaming service you stopped watching. An app that renewed quietly. A software subscription from two years ago. A gym membership from a resolution that did not last past February.

Small subscriptions feel harmless individually. Five different ten-dollar subscriptions are fifty dollars a month. Six hundred dollars a year. That money is leaving your account automatically and delivering almost nothing in return.

Go through every charge and ask yourself one question: if I had to consciously choose to pay for this today, would I? If the answer is no, cancel it.

This takes about thirty minutes and can free up real money immediately, without changing how you live your life in any meaningful way.

2. Sell things you already own

Look around your home with fresh eyes.

The bike that has not moved in two years. Clothes with tags still on them. Electronics sitting in drawers. Kitchen equipment used once. Old hobby supplies from something you moved on from. Furniture taking up space in a room you barely use.

These items have zero value sitting where they are. But to someone actively searching for them, they might be exactly what they need.

Platforms like Facebook Marketplace, eBay, and local selling groups make listing things straightforward. Pick five items this weekend. Take decent photos. Write clear descriptions. Post them. You are not building a business. You are converting unused things into payments.

One good weekend of selling can sometimes generate enough for a noticeable payment on a smaller debt.

3. Call your credit card company and ask for a lower rate

This takes about ten minutes and most people never do it.

Call the number on the back of your credit card. Tell them you have been a customer for some time, you have been paying consistently, and you would like to discuss lowering your interest rate.

They do not always say yes. But they say yes often enough that it is always worth asking. If you have a good payment history, there is a genuine chance they will reduce your rate, sometimes significantly.

A lower rate does not put more money in your pocket directly. But it means more of each future payment goes toward your actual balance rather than the bank's interest income. You did not increase your income. You made your existing payments work harder.

This one surprises people. Most assume the answer will be no and never call. But credit card companies do not want to lose paying customers. If you have been reliable, they have a reason to keep you. Ask.

4. Try one no-spend week

Not forever. One week.

For seven days, spend money only on essentials. Rent, utilities, groceries, transportation, medication. Everything else stops. No takeout, no online shopping, no impulse purchases, no small treats that add up.

At the end of the week, look at what is left. Take that amount and send it to your smallest debt before you can spend it on anything else.

The no-spend week does two things. It generates actual money for your debt. And it reveals, often uncomfortably clearly, exactly where your money disappears to in a normal week. That information is valuable. You do not need to live on no-spend weeks permanently. But doing one a month, and redirecting whatever you save, builds a consistent habit of finding extra money.

5. Use every unexpected payment for debt

Tax refund. Work bonus. Birthday money. Cash gifts. Rebates. Money from selling something. A payout from an old insurance claim.

All of it goes to the debt. Immediately, before it blends back into your regular spending.

The reason this works so well is that you were not counting on any of it. Your normal life does not depend on that money. So sending it directly to a credit card balance does not feel like a sacrifice. It feels like an accelerator.

A five hundred dollar tax refund applied to a small debt can clear it entirely, giving you a win months ahead of schedule. That win creates momentum. Momentum keeps you going.

6. Try cash for spending categories you overspend on

Cards make spending invisible. You tap, you move on, and you do not fully register what left your account until the statement arrives.

Cash creates a physical limit. When it is gone, it is gone.

Choose one or two categories where you tend to overspend. Eating out, entertainment, personal shopping. Decide on a weekly budget for each. Withdraw that amount in cash on your next pay day. When the cash runs out, you stop spending in that category until next week.

Most people spend significantly less when they use cash. Whatever is left in the envelope at the end of the week goes to the debt. No deliberating. No saving it for something else. Straight to the balance.

7. Find small ways to earn a little more

Sometimes reducing expenses is not enough. You need more coming in.

That does not mean building a business or working every available hour. It means finding temporary, targeted income with a specific purpose: paying off debt faster.

What do you already know how to do that someone else needs? Are there skills you use at your main job that translate to occasional freelance work? Is there something in your home you could rent out temporarily? Is there a service you could offer occasionally in your neighbourhood?

The key is keeping it focused. This extra income has one job. It goes to the debt. Once the debt is gone, the pressure to earn extra lifts. Knowing the timeline is temporary makes it easier to commit to.

Quick reference: seven ways to find extra money

  • Review and cancel unused subscriptions (thirty minutes, immediate results)
  • Sell unused items around your home (one weekend, one-off payment)
  • Call your credit card company and ask for a lower interest rate (ten minutes)
  • Try one no-spend week per month and redirect whatever you save
  • Apply all unexpected income directly to debt before spending it
  • Use cash envelopes for spending categories where you tend to overspend
  • Find small temporary income opportunities with a specific debt payoff purpose

The thing most people miss

When people think about finding extra money for debt, they look for one big source.

That is rarely how it works in practice. What actually happens is that several small sources combine. Cancelled subscriptions plus one good selling weekend plus a no-spend week plus a redirected bonus. None of them impressive individually. Together they become a meaningful extra payment every month.

Finding extra money is not a one-time event. It is an ongoing habit of looking for opportunities and redirecting them consistently. That consistency, month after month, is what actually clears the debt.

Common questions

How much extra do I actually need to make a difference?
Less than most people think. Even an extra thirty to fifty dollars a month on a small balance can clear it weeks or months faster than the minimum payment alone. The free tracker can show you exactly how much difference any specific amount makes for your own debts.
Should I save money at the same time as paying off debt?
A small emergency fund, usually five hundred to one thousand dollars, is worth having before going aggressively on debt. Beyond that, most financial advisors suggest prioritising debt repayment over savings when interest rates on the debt are higher than what savings accounts are offering, which is usually the case.
Is it better to cut expenses or earn more?
The most effective approach usually combines both. Cutting expenses creates immediate breathing room and can be done right now. Earning more creates long-term flexibility but takes more time and energy to set up. Start with expenses, then look for earning opportunities if you need more.
What if my income already barely covers my expenses?
Start with the subscription audit. That is the lowest-effort option and often finds money immediately. If that is not enough, the no-spend week can reveal spending you did not realise was happening. And a call to your credit card company costs nothing and can reduce the amount interest is taking from every payment.
